Drop a black hole into the solar system.

Worldline starts from the real solar system and lets you add black holes, neutron stars and planets, then watch general relativity play out: orbits turn, binaries spiral together as they radiate gravitational waves, black holes merge, and planets are torn apart by tides. Every physics model is checked against theory and observation.

What it does

The real solar system

It starts from NASA JPL's positions: the Sun, the planets, all 459 known moons, 62 dwarf planets, asteroids and comets, and 28,331 belt asteroids and Kuiper belt objects. Planets turn as the IAU's rotation models say, Saturn wears its rings as Cassini measured them, and the moons carry their spacecraft maps.

Run it for a year and Earth ends within 0.41 km of JPL's. Press Now and it shows the solar system as it is today.

Gravity as Einstein wrote it

Every massive body pulls on every other in relativistic N-body gravity (Einstein, Infeld and Hoffmann), with the higher-order terms that make close pairs lose energy to gravitational waves.

Mercury's perihelion turns 42.9807″ a century, where general relativity says 42.9805″. The Hulse–Taylor pulsar's orbit shrinks 1.0σ from the rate its timing measured.

The physics of close pairs

Gravitational waves, seen and heard

Pairs spiral together as they radiate, and the waves window plays their chirp. The overlays draw the waves' crests spiraling out at the speed of light, and how fast clocks run.

GW150914 replayed: two black holes spiral in from 20 Hz and merge into one of 63.03 Suns spinning at 0.683. LIGO measured 62 ± 4 Suns and 0.67.

Black-hole mergers

Black holes up close

Near a black hole, bodies move in its exact spacetime (Kerr's, when it spins), down to the innermost stable orbit. Its shadow and the sky bent around it are traced ray by ray.

From 570 km, Gaia BH1's shadow is 7.27° across, exactly as Synge's formula puts it.

Motion near a black hole

Stars held up by quantum mechanics

White dwarfs reach Chandrasekhar's limit, 1.4563 Suns for carbon and oxygen. Neutron stars, built from the SLy equation of state, top out at 2.048 Suns. Small black holes evaporate by Hawking radiation, labeled theoretical.

Up close, a neutron star is drawn as its own gravity bends its light: the Hulse–Taylor pulsar looks 1.25 times its size, with 67% of its surface in view.

Tides and collisions

A planet that nears a star or a black hole becomes a fluid of thousands of particles, stepped on the GPU, and tides pull it apart. A collision leaves what Leinhardt and Stewart's scaling laws say; a crater is as wide as Holsapple's give.

The Moon-forming impact, two planets of rock and iron, leaves 1.38 lunar masses in orbit, inside the 1.33 to 1.62 that Canup's simulations find. Whether that holds at higher resolution isn't shown yet.

Giant impacts

Eclipses and the sky

The eclipse finder searches the simulation itself. It finds all 24 solar and lunar eclipses of 2026 to 2030 in NASA's Five Millennium Canon, and no others, the worst 5.0 seconds off, and draws each one's path across the globe.

Stand anywhere on Earth to watch the sky. Through 2026, all 2,870 rises and sets of the Sun and Moon in the U.S. Naval Observatory's tables for Washington and Sydney come out to the minute.

The Milky Way

Leave the solar system: the nearest and brightest stars at their measured places and motions, Gaia's 21,480 stars within 50 parsecs, and 251 star clusters, nebulae and satellite galaxies.

The rest of the galaxy is 30,000 stars drawn in balance with its mass, and each feels anything heavy you drop among them.

The galaxy's stars

Famous events, as films

Twelve scenarios: Mercury's perihelion, the Hulse–Taylor and double pulsars, GW150914 and GW170817, a planet meeting a black hole, TON 618 swallowing Sagittarius A*, a white dwarf pushed past its limit, an evaporating black hole, the next total solar eclipse, the Moon-forming impact and a black hole through the Sun's neighborhood.

Each plays as a film: the camera moves shot by shot, captions tell the story, and cards beside the bodies set the published measurements next to what the simulation measures.

The scenarios

Honest about what it knows

Every body names the physics model moving it, and grades it: within range, approximate, or beyond its model. What's an illustration rather than a measurement says so. Where physics is unknown, inside a black hole or past the published equations of state, it's labeled, not invented. And the manual inside the app (F1) explains each model and where it stops being right.

Run it

Worldline is written in Rust. The Windows download comes with the v1 release; for now it builds from source.

  1. Install Rust from rustup.rs.
  2. Get the code and run it. The first build takes a few minutes. 
    git clone https://github.com/Bishnu2430/Worldline
cd Worldline
cargo run --release
  3. Drag to rotate, scroll to zoom, double-click a body to fly to it. Space runs or stops time, + and − change its speed, and F1 opens the manual, which starts at the beginning.

Some places to start:

cargo run --release -- --scenario gw150914      # two black holes merge
cargo run --release -- --scenario hulse-taylor  # the pulsar, as a film
cargo run --release -- --eclipse 7              # the eclipse of 12 August 2026
cargo run --release -- --manual                 # open the manual
cargo run --release -- --quality low            # for a weaker computer

To run the checks yourself: cargo test, and the slow ones with cargo test --release -- --ignored.

Developed on Windows 11 with an Intel Iris Xe. The continuous integration builds it and runs its tests on Linux.
